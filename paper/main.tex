\title{How far can anomalous electron screening reach at rest?
Constraints from beam measurements and null neutron searches}
\author{{[}Author names and affiliations to be added{]}}
\date{Draft, October 2026}

\begin{abstract}
Low-energy d(d,p)t measurements on deuterated metals imply electron
screening energies \(U_e\) of 0.2--0.8 keV, rising to 1.7 keV in
electrochemically loaded PdD (Karahadian et al.~2026), far above the
\textasciitilde25 eV of gas targets and atomic theory. These are beam
measurements: they probe d--d separations below \textasciitilde0.004 Å
at keV energies. Whether the anomaly also acts on deuterons \emph{at
rest} in the lattice is open, and is what a room-temperature fusion
effect would require. We answer it quantitatively. A log-grid Numerov
solver for the d--d relative-motion ground state, using the Pachucki
Born--Oppenheimer potential, reproduces the D\(_2\), HD and H\(_2\)
binding energies to ≤0.001 eV and the published molecular fusion rates.
We represent the anomalous screening as an extra potential
\(-\Delta U\,g(R/\ell)\) of unknown range \(\ell\), with four profile
shapes, its strength fixed by matching the measured \(U_e\) at the beam
turning point, and compute the fusion rate of lattice deuteron pairs as
a function of \(\ell\). The rate stays below \(10^{-100}\) s\(^{-1}\)
while the screening is short-ranged, then jumps when the extra
attraction binds a new d--d state at 0.07--0.24 Å. \textbf{(A)}
Requiring consistency with the null neutron limit for Pd and Ti at rest
(\(<2\times10^{-26}\) s\(^{-1}\) per pair, 1990) bounds the range to
\textbf{≈0.003--0.3 Å} across 112 model variants (profile shape, lattice
model, D--D spacing 0.94--2.75 Å, \(U_e\) = 0.17--2.0 keV). For
keV-scale screening with a long-tailed profile the allowed range
collapses to the beam turning point itself. \textbf{(B)} In every
variant, rates approaching 1 W cm\(^{-3}\) occur only at ranges already
excluded by the neutron limit: \emph{for screening-plus-confinement
mechanisms, watts imply neutrons.} We relate these results to the
Leggett--Baym equilibrium bound and propose three experiments that test
the remaining assumptions: a 5.5 MeV gamma search for neutron-free p+d
fusion at rest, beam screening in a heavy-fermion compound with a
temperature switch, and same-target p+d vs d+d screening.
\end{abstract}

\hypertarget{introduction}{%
\section{1. Introduction}\label{introduction}}

Room-temperature fusion claims in deuterated metals {[}Fleischmann--Pons
1989; Jones et al.~1989{]} were met within months by molecular rate
calculations {[}Koonin \& Nauenberg 1989{]}, general bounds on barrier
penetration in many-body systems at equilibrium {[}Leggett \& Baym
1989{]}, estimates of conduction-electron screening {[}Parmenter \& Lamb
1989{]}, and null neutron searches {[}Aberdam et al.~1990{]}. A large
re-examination found no evidence for the effect {[}Berlinguette et
al.~2019{]}.

One anomaly has since been firmly established by accelerator
experiments. Fitted to low-energy d(d,p)t yields on deuterated metals,
the \textbf{electron screening energy} is \(U_e \approx 200\)--800 eV in
many metals {[}Raiola et al.~2002, 2004; Czerski et al.{]}, against
\(\approx 25\) eV for D\(_2\) gas targets and the \(\approx 28\) eV
adiabatic limit. Recent work found a 15\% rate increase with
electrochemical loading {[}Chen et al.~2025{]} and fitted
\(U_e = 0.8 \pm 0.2\) keV for beam-loaded Pd, rising to \(1.7 \pm 0.2\)
keV with electrochemical loading (Ti: 0.3 → 0.9 keV), together with a
yield plateau below 2.5 keV {[}Karahadian et al.~2026{]}. The authors
note that \(U_e\) may lump several mechanisms, including depth-dependent
loading and near-surface replenishment of deuterium.

All of these are \textbf{beam measurements}: they fix the effective
potential near the classical turning point of keV deuterons,
\(R_{tp}\approx 0.004\) Å. They say nothing directly about deuterons at
rest, 1--3 Å apart in the lattice. That is the regime any
room-temperature effect would need. This paper asks: \emph{if the
anomalous screening extends to deuterons at rest, how far can it reach
without contradicting the null searches, and what would it take to give
practical rates?}

\hypertarget{method}{%
\section{2. Method}\label{method}}

\hypertarget{fusion-rate-of-a-bound-pair}{%
\subsection{2.1 Fusion rate of a bound
pair}\label{fusion-rate-of-a-bound-pair}}

For a bound pair, the fusion rate is
\[\Gamma = A\,|\psi(0)|^2, \qquad A = \frac{S(0)\,c}{\pi\alpha\,\mu c^2},\]
with \(S(0) = 109.3\) keV b (Bosch--Hale, both d+d branches), giving
\(A = 1.52\times10^{-16}\) cm\(^3\) s\(^{-1}\). \(\psi\) is the s-wave
ground state of the relative motion in potential \(V(R)\). We solve
\(u'' = 2\mu(V-E)u\) on a logarithmic grid from 0.5 fm, starting from
the regular repulsive-Coulomb solution, find the energy by node-counting
bisection, and track amplitudes in log space (\(\psi(0)\) is
\textasciitilde{}\(10^{-37}\) of its peak).

\hypertarget{lattice-pair-and-anomalous-screening}{%
\subsection{2.3 Lattice pair and anomalous
screening}\label{lattice-pair-and-anomalous-screening}}

A lattice d--d pair is modelled by Thomas--Fermi-screened repulsion
(0.57 Å for Pd) plus a restoring force on the separation set by the PdD
optical phonon (37 meV), around an equilibrium spacing \(d_0\). We use
\(d_0\) = 2.75 Å (octahedral neighbours) and also 1.4 and 0.94 Å, the
average and minimum equilibrium separations reported for heavily loaded
Pd {[}Sun \& Tománek 1989; values as quoted in later reviews{]}.
Variants with a barrier-capped restoring force, other screening lengths
and phonon energies are in the repository.

The anomalous screening is an extra potential
\(\Delta V(R) = -\Delta U\,g(R/\ell)\) with four shapes: exponential,
Gaussian, step and Yukawa-type (\(g(x) = (1-e^{-x})/x\), which has a
long \(-q/R\) tail). For each range \(\ell\), \(\Delta U\) is fixed by
requiring \(\Delta V(R_{tp}) = -U_{\mathrm{meas}}\), so every model
reproduces the beam data. When \(\Delta V\) creates a new bound state at
short range, the effective rate is the smaller of the bound-state fusion
rate and the WKB tunneling rate into it from the lattice site.

\hypertarget{results}{%
\section{3. Results}\label{results}}

\hypertarget{baseline}{%
\subsection{3.1 Baseline}\label{baseline}}

A d--d pair at the lattice spacing fuses at \(10^{-123.9}\) s\(^{-1}\)
(2.75 Å), \(10^{-112.1}\) s\(^{-1}\) (1.4 Å) and \(10^{-109.5}\)
s\(^{-1}\) (0.94 Å). One watt per cm\(^3\) of PdD requires
\textasciitilde{}\(6\times10^{-11}\) s\(^{-1}\) per pair, so the
baseline gap is 98--114 orders of magnitude. For comparison, a D\(_2\)
molecule (0.74 Å) gives \(2\times10^{-64}\) s\(^{-1}\). Compressing a
molecule-like pair, every hydrogen-isotope fuel except p+p reaches watt
level at a spacing of 7.3--8.2 pm, corresponding to a screening particle
\textasciitilde9× heavier than an electron.

\subsection{3.2 The cliff}\label{the-cliff}}

While the extra screening is confined to the region the beams probe, the
lattice rate stays below \(10^{-100}\) s\(^{-1}\). As \(\ell\) grows,
the rate \textbf{jumps} when \(\Delta V\) binds a new d--d state, at
0.24, 0.13 and 0.07 Å for \(U_{\mathrm{meas}}\) = 165, 300 and 575 eV
(exponential profile, 2.75 Å). It is then limited by tunneling into that
state and by fusion within it (\(10^{-27}\) to \(10^{-7}\) s\(^{-1}\)).

Across 112 variants the allowed range is \textbf{≈0.003--0.3 Å}. It is
set mainly by the profile shape and the Thomas--Fermi length; lattice
stiffness, phonon energy and tunneling attempt frequency
(×10\(^{\pm3}\)) change it by ≲20\%, and closer equilibrium spacings
tighten it. \textbf{For keV-scale screening with a long-tailed (Yukawa)
profile, the bound reaches the beam turning point itself
(\textasciitilde0.003--0.004 Å):} keV screening cannot extend
appreciably beyond the distances the beam probes. Step profiles and the
weakest screening (165 eV) never reach the limit and remain
unconstrained, but those cases also never come within 20 orders of watt
level.

\hypertarget{claim-b-watts-imply-neutrons}{%
\subsection{3.4 Claim B: watts imply
neutrons}\label{claim-b-watts-imply-neutrons}}

Of the 72 variants in notebook 04b (and the 40 in notebook 04), 33 reach
1 W cm\(^{-3}\) somewhere on the beam-consistent curve, all with
\(U_{\mathrm{meas}} \ge 575\) eV. \textbf{In every one, the neutron
limit is crossed at a shorter range than watt level, and no variant
jumps from below the limit straight to watt level.} The smallest margin,
a factor of 1.29 in range, occurs for the Yukawa profile at 1.7 keV. The
physical reason is that a rate climbing from \(10^{-26}\) to
\(10^{-10}\) s\(^{-1}\) must pass through the excluded band. Only a
discontinuous jump, which the bound-state kinetics never produce, could
skip it. Any screening-plus-confinement mechanism that delivered watts
at rest would therefore have produced neutron rates exceeding the 1990
limit by \textasciitilde15 orders of magnitude.

\hypertarget{the-neutron-free-channel}{%
\subsection{3.5 The neutron-free
channel}\label{the-neutron-free-channel}}

p+d → ³He + γ (5.49 MeV) has no neutron branch, so the 1990 limit does
not constrain it directly. With the same beam-consistent screening (up
to 575 eV), the p+d lattice rate peaks at \(10^{-12.3}\) s\(^{-1}\),
\textasciitilde2.5 orders short of watt level even in a 50/50 H/D mix,
and coexisting d+d pairs violate the neutron limit first. 1 W of p+d
would also emit \textasciitilde{}\(10^{12}\) gammas per second.
Disposing of fusion energy without any radiation would require the
reaction energy to go directly into the lattice. For any process through
the excited ⁴He compound nucleus, suppressing neutrons even by
\(10^{-3}\) requires a lattice decay width exceeding the 23.85 MeV
transition energy, and coherent transfer is causality-limited (light
travels \textless0.003 Å in the compound lifetime). We do not consider
such channels further.

\hypertarget{relation-to-prior-work}{%
\section{4. Relation to prior work}\label{relation-to-prior-work}}

\textbf{Leggett \& Baym {[}1989{]}} showed that, at equilibrium, the
d--d tunneling rate is bounded by its Born--Oppenheimer value. Strictly,
then, the anomalous screening cannot act on deuterons in equilibrium at
rest at all. Our analysis is complementary. It does not assume
equilibrium or a particular microscopic mechanism; it takes the
\emph{measured} beam anomaly at face value, allows it to extend to rest
with an unknown range, and asks what the null searches allow. The
answer, a range of order 0.01--0.3 Å and at most the beam turning point
for keV screening, is an empirical constraint that holds even if the
equilibrium assumption fails (e.g.~under loading or irradiation).
Leggett \& Baym also argued that strongly reduced short-range repulsion
would bind alpha particles to the metal more strongly; our short-range
bound states (binding of order eV) would face the same thermochemical
test.

\textbf{Czerski, Heide \& Huke {[}2006{]}} extrapolated effective
screening energies to cold-fusion energies and compared them with
positive electrolysis claims; we instead model the range of the
screening and confront it with null data. \textbf{Parmenter \& Lamb
{[}1989{]}} estimated \textasciitilde{}\(10^{-30}\) s\(^{-1}\) from
conduction-electron screening. \textbf{Huke et al.~{[}2008{]}} and the
Lipoglavšek group identified target and depth-profile effects in \(U_e\)
extraction, which motivate experiment E3. The sub-keV plateau of
\textbf{Karahadian et al.~{[}2026{]}} (\textgreater{}\(10^{18}\) above
bare-nucleus expectations at 0.25 keV beam energy) is not described by a
constant \(U_e\), as the authors note; our bounds use their fitted
values above 2.5 keV.

\hypertarget{proposed-experiments}{%
\section{5. Proposed experiments}\label{proposed-experiments}}

\textbf{E1: gamma search for p+d fusion at rest.} A 10 g Pd foil
co-loaded with H and D (0.45/0.45), with steady and cycled loading, a
shielded HPGe/LaBr\(_3\) detector with muon veto, and H-only, D-only and
unloaded controls; 30 days. With assumed backgrounds this reaches
\textasciitilde{}\(10^{-26}\) (surface) and \(4\times10^{-28}\)
(underground) per p--d pair per s, excluding screening ranges
≳0.12--0.16 Å for 300--575 eV in a channel the neutron searches did not
cover.

\textbf{E2: heavy-fermion screening with a temperature switch.} d(d,p)t
at \(E_d\) = 5--30 keV on a deuterium-implanted heavy-fermion compound
(Ce- or Yb-based) and its non-f analogue, at 4 K and 300 K, with in-situ
depth profiling (ERDA). A ``heavy-electron'' origin of the anomaly
predicts a large rise only below the coherence temperature; standard
theory predicts no change. About 6 h per point at 10 μA.

\textbf{E3: same-target p+d vs d+d screening.} Alternating
H\(^+\)/D\(^+\) beams matched in stopping range on one deuterated target
separate true electron screening (equal \(U_e\)), depth-profile
artifacts (range-dependent) and a d+d-specific nuclear resonance
(\(U_e^{dd} > U_e^{pd}\)). A 5σ answer needs \textasciitilde2.6\% p+d
statistics, about 1.5 days per point at 1 mA.

\hypertarget{limitations}{%
\section{6. Limitations}\label{limitations}}

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{(\roman{enumi})}
\tightlist
\item
  The screening profiles are phenomenological; four shapes span short-
  and long-tailed behaviour but are not derived. (ii) The lattice pair
  is a single radial pair; anisotropy, many-body exchange and specific
  defect geometries are not modelled, although lattice stiffness and
  spacing affect the bound only weakly. (iii) The bound relies on one
  null measurement {[}Aberdam et al.~1990{]}; the weaker limit of
  Badurek et al.~(3.6×10⁻²³) loosens it only slightly. (iv) Several
  literature inputs were verified at abstract level; the repository's
  VERIFICATION.md lists the status of each. (v) The p+d analysis was
  carried to 575 eV only.
\end{enumerate}

\hypertarget{conclusions}{%
\section{7. Conclusions}\label{conclusions}}

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  Matching the measured beam screening (0.3--2.0 keV) and extending it
  to deuterons at rest, the null neutron searches bound its range to
  \textbf{≈0.003--0.3 Å}; for keV screening with a long-tailed profile,
  to the beam turning point itself.
\item
  In every model variant, rates approaching 1 W cm\(^{-3}\) lie inside
  the region already excluded by the neutron searches: \textbf{watts
  imply neutrons.}
\item
  The anomaly is therefore either confined to short range (and
  irrelevant at rest), or it is something other than static screening,
  which experiments E1--E3 are designed to tell apart.
\end{enumerate}
